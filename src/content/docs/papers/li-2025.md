**Source.** *Training Language Models to Explain Their Own Computations* ([Li, Guo, Huang, Steinhardt & Andreas, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08579)), Transluce and MIT, arXiv preprint (v3, February 2026). Not peer reviewed. Code: [github.com/TransluceAI/introspective-interp](https://github.com/TransluceAI/introspective-interp).

**Description.** Li and colleagues ask whether a language model can learn to describe its own internal computations, and whether it does so more accurately than a different model trained for the same job. Ground truth comes from three interpretability procedures run on a fixed target model: automated descriptions of sparse autoencoder (SAE) features, the outcomes of activation patching, and the outcomes of removing a hint from the input. Explainer models are fine-tuned to reproduce these explanations. An explainer that is the target model itself is compared with explainers built from other models, a nearest-neighbor baseline and zero-shot self-interpretation. The paper states the **privileged access hypothesis**: models trained to explain their own computations do so more accurately than other models trained to explain them.

## Measurement card

| Stage | What the paper reports |
|---|---|
| Measurand | Accuracy of an explainer's predictions about three properties of the target's computation: what activates a feature, whether and how the output changes under patching, and whether and how the answer changes when a hint is removed. The comparative quantity is self-explainer minus other explainers. |
| Procedure | Models, SAE source, held-out features, data construction, layer blocks, the balancing of changed and unchanged cases, fine-tuning settings and all prompts are given. |
| Instrument and indication | A fine-tuned explainer that receives the target's activation vector as a token at its input layer and outputs text: a free-text description for features, or a templated sentence saying whether the output changes and to what. |
| Measurement model | Feature descriptions are scored by an LLM judge against a gold label and by how well the description predicts true activations. Patching and ablation outputs are scored by exact match, macro-F1 over changed and unchanged, and content match. Changed and unchanged cases are balanced so that guessing the majority does not score well. |
| Calibration | Patching and ablation are scored against the target's own counterfactual outputs. Feature descriptions are scored against Neuronpedia labels, which the paper's error analysis blames for 43.5% of zero-scored cases. The judge is checked against one human annotator on 100 pairs. Code is linked; the constructed data and outputs are not stated as released. |
| Uncertainty | Explainer model, fine-tuning method, projection initialization, training-set size, target model and explainer inputs are varied, with standard errors and paired t-tests. The self advantage holds across targets and tasks, but for Llama-3.1-8B features, two closely related Llama explainers score within error of the self-explainer. Training seeds are not reported. |
| Comparability | Self versus other is the core comparison: the same target explained by itself, related and unrelated models, a nearest-neighbor lookup and zero-shot self-interpretation. Activation similarity between explainer and target is measured and related to performance. |
| Validity conditions | Explainers trained on SAE features are tested on full activations and activation differences, and a second target (Gemma-2-9B) is run. The paper notes that the fine-tuned explainer is not the target model in the strict sense. Open-weight models from 8B to 70B, ordinary forward passes and public datasets. |

## Design

1. **Framework.** An interpretability procedure answers a question about the fixed target model, and the explainer is fine-tuned to reproduce the answer.
2. **Feature descriptions.** Descriptions of Llama-3.1-8B SAE features come from Neuronpedia. The feature vector is fed to the explainer as a token; explainers of another size get it through a learned projection. 1,550 features are held out.
3. **Activation patching.** Counterfactual pairs from CounterFact are run through the target with block-averaged activations patched in; the explainer predicts whether the output changes and to what.
4. **Input ablation.** MMLU questions carry a hint naming one option; the explainer predicts whether removing it changes the answer and to what.
5. **Comparisons.** Trained explainers from several models, a nearest-neighbor baseline, zero-shot self-interpretation (SelfIE), and the untrained target prompted zero-shot.
6. **Scoring.** An LLM judge (GPT-4.1-mini) and a simulator score for features; exact match, F1 and content match for patching and ablation.

## Results

- For Llama-3.1-8B features, the self-explainer scores 76.2 on the judge; Llama-3-8B scores 77.0 and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct 77.1, within error; Qwen3-8B scores 70.3, nearest neighbors 58.5 and SelfIE 40.1.
- The 70B explainer does 14% better starting from a pre-trained projection than from a random one. The paper proposes that activation similarity between explainer and target predicts performance.
- Llama learns from little data: at 0.8% of the training features it reaches 71–89% of its final performance, against 24–66% for Qwen.
- For activation patching on Qwen3-8B, the self-explainer reaches 64.0% exact match against 54.1% for Llama-3.1-8B and 5.0% for the untrained model.
- For input ablation on Qwen3-8B, 83.4% against 58.1% and 8.9%. The untrained model almost always predicts no change.
- With Llama-3.1-8B as target, the self advantage is smaller: 48.6 against 41.7 for patching and 63.8 against 56.7 for ablation.
- Removing the activation from the explainer's input lowers patching accuracy from 64.0 to 59.9; removing the layer or token has negligible effect.
- The judge agrees with a human annotator 81.25% of the time.
