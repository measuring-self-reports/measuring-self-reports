---
title: Blandfort & Pawar 2026
description: Tests whether a model's predictions of its own behavioral rates are specific to that model or equally available from generic and other-model baselines.
---

**Source.** *Strangers to Themselves: What Language Models Say About Themselves Is Generic* ([Blandfort & Pawar, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09899)), arXiv preprint. Not peer reviewed. Code, measurements and predictions: [github.com/PredictablyWeird/strangers-to-themselves](https://github.com/PredictablyWeird/strangers-to-themselves); the full experimental record is on Zenodo.

**Description.** Blandfort and Pawar treat a model's statement about how it would behave as a prediction, and score it against the rate at which the model actually behaves that way. Across nine public behavioral evaluations, each condition (an MMLU subject, a demographic cell, an agentic task) has a measured rate. Fifteen prediction methods estimate those rates while varying three things independently: how much the predictor is told, who the question is about (the model itself, capable AI agents in general, or other models), and whether it asks for a rate or a forced choice. Self-specific knowledge would show up as a gap between asking the model about itself and the controls that remove the self. Twelve model entries from six labs; a fine-tuning arm uses five open models.

## Measurement card

| Stage | What the paper reports |
|---|---|
| Measurand | Self-knowledge as prediction: the correlation across conditions between predicted and measured rates, compared between self-directed questions and controls. A separate estimate ranks evaluations by how much model-specific signal exists to be found. |
| Procedure | Samples per condition, grading, position-bias control, bootstrap and permutation settings, method settings and prompt templates are given. Elicitation is at temperature 0; behavior is measured under each evaluation's own sampling. Provider strings and query dates are listed. |
| Instrument and indication | A 0–100 percentage, an A/B forced choice, a per-item probability averaged over a condition, or a sampled proxy rate. The framework assumes asking does not change behaving. Reasoning mode changes whether some models answer at all. |
| Measurement model | Pearson r for pattern; mean absolute error and signed bias for level; ceiling-normalized r; partial correlation net of the cross-model mean. Several instruments address response bias: signed bias, the shift against the generic control, a list experiment, randomized response and a language-register sweep. |
| Calibration | The standard is behavior measured on public evaluations. A fixed grader judges sampled responses, and a different grader handles its own model's cells, so no model grades itself. Every number is generated by scripts from committed results, and the preregistration is frozen in the repository. |
| Uncertainty | Bootstrap intervals over model-by-evaluation cells and paired sign-flip tests. Information, subject, form, register, reasoning mode, model tier, metric, split and hindsight tuning are varied. Nine of thirteen pre-specified outcomes replicated as pinned and one partially. |
| Comparability | Asking, watching (proxy scenarios), an outside analyst and the cross-model behavior mean, which consults no model under test, all target the same rates. Self against generic subject and against other models is the core design. |
| Validity conditions | Nine public evaluations and low-effort reasoning settings. On agentic evaluations the item-informed method sees only the opening state, and the controls cannot detect evaluation awareness. The target is a rate over conditions, which the paper distinguishes from single-prompt self-simulation. |

## Design

1. **Behavior.** Nine evaluations: DiscrimEval, PropensityBench, Capability (MMLU accuracy), Sycophancy, Reward hacking, two τ² policy tasks, MASK under pressure and Agentic misalignment. Each condition's rate is measured from repeated samples.
2. **Prediction methods.** Fifteen methods. Abstract asking gives only a description of the situation; informed asking adds the model's measured history or the actual items; watching re-measures the model on generated proxy scenarios. Each asking method has a generic-subject control, and outside-view methods never consult the model under test.
3. **Scoring.** Correlation between predicted and measured rates per model and evaluation, averaged, with a noise ceiling for each cell.
4. **Splits and preregistration.** Settings are chosen on a development pool of six small models and frozen; headline results come from a test split scored once, with claims pre-specified.
5. **Self-specificity tests.** Generic subject against self; other models' self-predictions; identity transfer, scoring each model's answers against every model's behavior; partial correlation net of the cross-model mean; a fixed analyst model given the same history.
6. **Scale.** Frontier and small models compared on the single-turn evaluations.
7. **Fine-tuning.** Base models fine-tuned on records of their own measured behavior, and a Binder-style property-prediction fine-tune for comparison.

## Results

- Direct self-report is weak (r = +0.04); showing the model the exact items raises it to +0.24.
- Asking about capable agents in general does as well (+0.28 against +0.24).
- Other models' self-predictions predict the target better than its own (+0.35 against +0.24).
- Answers fit the answerer's own behavior at +0.27 and other models' at +0.25, a self advantage of +0.02.
- An outside analyst with the same history does better than the model (+0.28 against +0.19).
- Net of the cross-model mean, direct self-report adds nothing (+0.04, interval including zero).
- Self-reports understate misbehavior: mean signed bias −0.19, reaching −0.42 on PropensityBench.
- Frontier models' self-reports are not significantly better than small models'; the authors call this a null, not evidence of equivalence.
- A Binder-style fine-tune raises Llama-3.3-70B's single-prompt self-prediction from 45.8% to 73.6% but barely moves the dispositional targets.

## Related papers

- Follows [Binder et al. 2025](/measuring-self-reports/papers/binder-2025/) in the fine-tuning arm, and argues that its cross-model behavior baseline is a stronger control than a trained imitator.
- Contrasts its scale result with [Lindsey 2025](/measuring-self-reports/papers/lindsey-2025/): no corresponding trend for self-description across contexts.
- [Moran & Whiting 2026](/measuring-self-reports/papers/moran-2026/) reach a similar conclusion for confidence.
- Cites [Betley et al. 2025](/measuring-self-reports/papers/betley-2025/) and [Kadavath et al. 2022](/measuring-self-reports/papers/kadavath-2022/).
